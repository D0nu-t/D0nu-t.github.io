Abstract

Comparing distance measures in multidimensional bounded-confidence models involves choices that are easy to leave unstated. We follow five of them, each met while auditing our own model. In an agent-based model of AI governance, a draft finding that geometry reverses the effect of enforcement lag came from reading each geometry against a benchmark; the test that compares the geometries keeps its sign from the draft's model to the audited one (interaction +0.034 and +0.034). The point toward which low trust pulls the perceived policy, which nobody had measured, moved the cosine result across that benchmark and, for one of four defensible targets, removed the difference between geometries. In a minimal Deffuant model, one shared threshold admits very different shares of opinion pairs under Euclidean, cosine and angular distance; matching the threshold at the start leaves interaction rates apart, and matching it over the run equalises them but not the number of opinion groups, so each matched point answers a different question. Counting groups with each geometry's own radius made two identical gates look different. We close with a six-item audit list.

1 Introduction

Bounded-confidence (BC) models let two agents influence each other only when their opinions are close enough (Deffuant et al., 2000; Hegselmann & Krause, 2002). With several opinion dimensions, "close enough" needs a distance, and comparing distances has become a modelling question of its own. Such a comparison involves five choices that can each create an effect or hide one:

  1. which test is read: each condition against a benchmark, or the comparison itself;
  2. where the origin sits, for angular gates and for any step that shrinks a quantity toward a point;
  3. the threshold value given to each distance measure;
  4. when coverage is matched: at the start of a run or over the run;
  5. how outcomes are counted: the radius used to group final opinions, and when the run is stopped.

We met every one of them while auditing our own model. In an unpublished draft we reported that, in an agent-based model of AI governance, geometry reverses the effect of enforcement lag on shocks. The first two choices produced that reading (Section 3). Section 4 then takes the other three in a minimal Deffuant model, and Section 6 collects the lessons into an audit list. The note is pre-emptive: we do not estimate how often published studies make these choices. A longer version with every per-cell result, the gate × update-step crossing and a fuller literature review also exists.

Related work

Lorenz (2008) compared the L1 and L∞ norms after rescaling the threshold so that their confidence regions had equal volume, and Li et al. (2026) note that lowering a topic weight at a fixed bound enlarges the region of agreement as well as changing its shape. We extend volume matching to coverage, the share of actual opinion pairs a gate admits, which also works for angular gates whose regions have no finite volume, and then to matching over the run. Flache et al. (2017) call for systematic comparisons of social-influence models; model docking (Axtell et al., 1996; Wilensky & Rand, 2007) showed that settings that look like implementation details carry substantive assumptions, and Gilbert et al. (2018) argue that a policy model's assumptions must be visible to be useful. The origin audit, coverage and pass-rate matching, and the common ruler are, as far as we know, new; testing the interaction (Gelman & Stern, 2006) and using equivalence tests (Lakens, 2017) restate standard advice in this setting.

2 Background

A geometry is a distance measure, used by the gate, together with its update step. For opinion vectors xi, xj we use Euclidean distance ‖xi − xj‖, cosine distance 1 − cos θij and the angle θij between them. The gate lets two agents converse when their distance is at most the threshold ε; the listener then moves a fraction r of the way toward the speaker, along a straight line for Euclidean distance and by rotating its opinion, keeping its length, for the two angular geometries. Because cosine distance is a strictly increasing function of the angle, a cosine gate at ε and an angular gate at arccos(1 − ε) admit exactly the same pairs: once matched, the two can differ only through their update steps and how outcomes are measured.

A gate's coverage is the share of opinion pairs within its threshold at one moment, and its pass rate the share of attempted conversations that pass it during a run. A comparison can give every geometry Euclidean's threshold number (shared), the threshold that gives the same coverage at the start (start-matched), or the threshold that gives the same pass rate over the run (run-matched).

Euclidean distance and the Euclidean step are unchanged by shifting and rescaling the opinion space; angular gates and steps are not, since they measure direction from the origin. The same holds for multiplicative shrinkage of a signal (such as a policy scaled by trust), decay toward zero, clipping after a non-equivariant step, zero initial values and ratio outcomes. If opinions are coded in [0,1]d, all of these refer to the corner where every dimension is at its minimum.

3 The test and the origin, in a governance model

Model and claim

PolicySwarm (Addala, 2026) models how a population's views on AI regulation respond to a policy, to media and to shocks (Appendix B). Agents hold four-dimensional opinions on a Barabási–Albert network and converse under a bounded-confidence gate. After an enforcement lag, each agent is pulled toward the policy as it perceives it: ai + τi(p + bi − ai) + η, where p is the policy, τi ∈ [0,1] the agent's trust, bi its ideological bias, η noise and ai the trust target, the point a low-trust agent's perceived policy is pulled toward.

The experiment compares paired runs with and without a schedule of shocks, which pull their targets toward the neutral centre and so lower the final variance of opinions. The relative shock effect is that change in variance divided by the variance of the run without shocks, and it is negative. Runs are made at enforcement lags of 0, 20 and 40 ticks. For each geometry the lag index is the mean relative shock effect at lag 40 as a percentage of its mean at lag 0, by size. The interaction is cosine's change from lag 0 to lag 40 minus Euclidean's, paired by seed; with negative effects, a positive interaction means lag enlarges the effect less under cosine, so that cosine attenuates the lag effect. The index is a proportional change within one geometry, and the interaction compares the two on one scale; since cosine's lag-0 effect is about half Euclidean's, the two can disagree, and we rest claims about geometry on the interaction.

The draft reported, on the absolute shock effect, an index of about 130 under Euclidean distance and about 75 under cosine distance, and called it a reversal. Read as policy advice, whether delays in enforcement make a population more or less fragile after shocks would have hinged on an untested modelling choice. An internal audit found the problem before any external use.

Design

We ran the audited engine with the draft's choices restored as switches: the gate measured from the cube's corner or its centre, the threshold shared or matched, the policy pull applied only after a conversation that passes the gate (as in the draft) or every tick, and four trust targets (the corner, the centre, the population's mean starting opinion, and the agent's own opinion). We also ran the draft's own engine with the audit's other fixes undone in turn, and a level-shift control that moves every agent's perceived policy by the corner's average shift, −(1 − τ̄), separating a shift in the mean from one that differs between trusting and distrustful agents. The recommended design uses a centred gate, a run-matched threshold and a pull applied every tick, which removes both of the draft's couplings. Every cell crosses Euclidean and cosine with the three lags over 100 paired seeds. We report each lag index with a paired bootstrap 95% interval and the interaction with a 95% t interval, and call an interaction equivalent to zero when its 90% interval lies within ±0.01; we set this margin after an earlier version had reported the result it is used on, so that test confirms a result already known.

Results

Demo 2

Lag index and interaction

The test

Across all 30 configurations the interaction is positive in 25, negative in 3 and not significantly different from zero in 2 (Figure 1). In the draft's own engine and settings it is +0.034 [+0.025, +0.044], the same direction as in the audited model. What looked like a reversal was cosine's index falling below 100 (78 on the absolute effect the draft used, against 132 for Euclidean) while the comparison between geometries kept its sign. Reading each geometry against 100 asks whether each effect grows with lag; the claim was about the difference between geometries, and only the interaction tests that (Gelman & Stern, 2006). Two of the negative cells come from an uncentred gate with a matched threshold and the gated pull, where the matched cosine thresholds are tiny because all opinions point into one orthant; the third is a trust target (below).

Which comparison isolates the threshold

The centred cells hold the gate fixed and vary only the threshold rule. With the pull applied every tick, the interaction barely moves across the shared, start-matched and run-matched rules (+0.035, +0.032, +0.034). In the unit cube the same change of threshold moves it from +0.038 to +0.020, and with the gated pull from +0.018 to −0.057: the threshold matters here mainly through the uncentred gate.

The origin

With trust shrinking toward the corner and angles measured from the corner, as in the draft, the audited engine gives Euclidean 116 and cosine 85, matching the draft's engine (118 [110, 127] and 85 [81, 88]). Shrinking toward the centre lifts cosine to 117; measuring the gate from the centre lifts it to 106. In this setting Euclidean's index barely moves with the trust target, and the level shift takes cosine only to 103: most of the corner's effect comes from shifting distrustful agents further than trusting ones, which an angle measured from the corner registers (Demo 1). The interaction stays positive throughout (+0.026, +0.018, +0.018): the origin moved cosine's index across 100 but left the sign of the comparison unchanged.

The trust target is an assumption

Trust has to shrink the perceived policy toward something, and the four targets give different answers (Table 1). The status quo behaves like the centre, as it must when starting opinions are uniform. Shrinking toward the corner leaves lag little effect under cosine (106). Shrinking toward the agent's own opinion makes the interaction equivalent to zero (+0.001, 90% interval [−0.003, +0.006]), and in the draft's setting the same target makes it negative (−0.019). Across the four targets, Euclidean's lag index alone runs from 113 to 147. The right target depends on how distrust distorts a policy signal, which the model cannot decide, so a study using such a model should state the target and vary it.

The remaining effect

In the recommended design with trust shrinking toward the centre, lag enlarges the shock effect under both geometries and less under cosine (indices 146 [138, 154] and 134 [129, 139]; interaction +0.034 [+0.027, +0.042]). We would report this result, with the condition the target sweep attaches to it.

4 The other three choices, in a minimal model

Design

A minimal Deffuant model (Appendix A) has 500 agents on an Erdős–Rényi graph, random matchings each round and μ = 0.3. The factorial crosses the opinion space (unit or centred cube), the dimension (d ∈ {2, 4, 8}) and a reference coverage (5%, 15%, 30%): 18 cells, 100 paired seeds each. Euclidean distance runs at the threshold giving the reference coverage at the start; cosine and angular distance run under the shared, start-matched and run-matched rules, and under an early-matched rule that matches the pass rate over the first 100 rounds only. Matched thresholds were found by bisection on 50 separate pilot seeds and checked on the evaluation seeds. The primary outcome is the number of opinion groups holding at least 1% of agents after 2,000 rounds, counted with one ruler for every geometry. We call a gap equivalent to zero when its 90% interval lies within ±0.02 for the pass rate or ±0.25 on the log2 scale (Lakens, 2017). Cosine and angular are reported separately, but they agree closely, so the text gives cosine and Table 3 both.

Results

The threshold value

One shared number admits very different shares of pairs: in the unit cube at ε = 0.5, a cosine gate admits 94% of pairs in four dimensions against 14% for Euclidean distance (Table 2). As the dimension grows, distances concentrate (Aggarwal et al., 2001):

Proposition 1 (coverage at a fixed threshold). For independent uniform opinions, as d → ∞, ‖xi − xj‖ / √d converges to 1/√6 in [0,1]d and to √(2/3) in [−1,1]d; cos θij converges to 3/4 in [0,1]d and to 0 in [−1,1]d. Hence at a fixed threshold, cosine and angular coverage tend to 0 or 1 according to which side of these limits the threshold falls, and Euclidean coverage tends to 0 unless the threshold grows like √d.

Each limit follows from the law of large numbers applied to the coordinate-wise sums; a threshold exactly at an angular limit, such as cosine at ε = 1 in the centred cube, admits half of all pairs in every dimension. In the minimal model a shared threshold leaves the pass rate apart by a median of 0.258 (cosine), equivalent to Euclidean's in no cell.

When coverage is matched

Start-matching leaves the pass rate apart by a median of 0.162: coverage drifts as opinions converge, faster under Euclidean distance than under the angular geometries, whose steps rotate an opinion without shortening it. Run-matching equalises the pass rate (equivalent in 16 of 18 cells) but not the number of groups (5 of 18; median gap 0.70 on the log2 scale); early-matching brings the pass rates much closer than start-matching but leaves the group counts as far apart as run-matching does (Table 3). At equal pass rates the geometries' response curves do not coincide (Figure 2), so the gap depends on where on the curves one compares them. Matching is therefore a choice of estimand: a start- or early-matched comparison includes a geometry's effect on how often agents interact, and a run-matched one excludes it. Our first draft reported that run-matching removed the gap because it counted the effective number of groups, an outcome that, once groups have formed, the pass rate nearly fixes.

Gate and update step

Run-matched, the angular geometries still leave final opinions more spread out than Euclidean distance does (median variance gap 0.57 on the log2 scale, positive in every cell). Crossing the gate with the update step shows where that comes from. With start-matched gates, a Euclidean gate with the cosine step raises the variance by +1.09 [+1.04, +1.14], most of the +1.44 [+1.38, +1.50] of the pure cosine pairing, while a cosine gate with the Euclidean step raises it by only +0.43 [+0.39, +0.46]; with run-matched cosine gates the cosine gate with the Euclidean step even lowers it (−1.04 [−1.14, −0.95]). The spread of opinion directions follows the gate rather than the step when gates are start-matched (+0.23 [+0.21, +0.24] against +0.08 [+0.07, +0.09]), and the gate and step together when they are run-matched. So the overall spread is mostly the update step's doing, and a comparison that changes the gate and the step at once cannot tell the two apart.

The ruler and the stopping time

Our first draft also grouped final opinions within a fraction of each run's own ε. Start-matched cosine and angular gates admit exactly the same pairs, yet under that rule their group counts are equivalent in only 10 of 18 cells; with one common ruler they are equivalent in all 18, so the apparent difference came from the ruler. After 500 rounds 44% of Euclidean runs are still changing, and after 4,000 13%, but the group gaps barely move over that range (start-matched: 0.93 to 0.87), so the remaining difference is not a transient; only reporting the stopping time and checking a longer run shows that.

5 Consequences for model comparisons

For anyone comparing distance measures, each of the five choices can produce or remove a difference on its own. In our two models a shared threshold changed the pass rate by a median of 0.177 to 0.258 of attempted conversations, a geometry-specific ruler left two identical gates in agreement in only 10 of 18 cells, and a test read against a benchmark turned an attenuation into a reported reversal. All three arose without a coding error, and a results table shows none of them unless the choice is reported.

For models used to inform policy, the case study shows a policy-relevant conclusion resting on choices its readers could not see. Whether enforcement delays make a population more or less fragile after shocks depended on the test read, on where the opinion space put its origin, and on a trust target that no one had measured. Gilbert et al. (2018) argue that a policy model is useful only when its assumptions are visible, and any trust or credibility parameter that shrinks or scales a signal carries an origin with it.

The choices are not specific to distance measures. Any comparison of model variants that share a free parameter on different scales, shrink a quantity toward a point, or count outcomes with a variant-specific rule meets the same problems, which is the docking problem (Axtell et al., 1996; Wilensky & Rand, 2007) in a narrower form. We have not measured how often published comparisons make these choices; the audit list below gives a way to check.

6 An audit list

Before reporting that a distance measure changes a result

  1. Test the comparison. A claim that a geometry changes an effect is an interaction; test it directly (Gelman & Stern, 2006), and support "no difference" with an equivalence test (Lakens, 2017).
  2. Audit the origin. Recode the opinion space by a shift and rescale and rerun a few seeds; any outcome that changes depends on the origin. Centre the space at a meaningful neutral point or say why not, and treat every shrinkage or decay target as an explicit, varied assumption.
  3. Do not rely on a shared threshold value. If one is used, show that the pass rates match.
  4. Report the response to ε, not one matched point. Start-, early- and run-matched points answer different questions; name the one you report, and check calibrated thresholds on separate seeds.
  5. Check for monotone equivalence. Distance measures related by a strictly increasing function are one gate once matched.
  6. Report the ruler and the stopping time. Count groups with one rule for every geometry, report how many runs have not settled, and check a longer run.

Applying the list costs a fraction of the comparison itself: a few reruns or a recount of saved states, about ten thresholds per geometry for the response curves, and one bisection per cell for run-matching.

7 Limitations

Opinions start uniform and independent, and we study pairwise Deffuant updates only, without varying μ, the population size or the network. Runs stop at 4,000 rounds, when 14% of runs have not settled. The case study rests on one model whose trust mechanism is specific to it, while the general point (multiplicative steps inherit the meaning of the origin) is not. The five choices are not the only hidden ones: the network, the update order and the population size are choices of the same kind.

Every figure, table and number in the paper is rebuilt by one script from the saved results; none is typed by hand.

Appendices

A. The minimal model

Agents hold opinions in [0,1]d or [−1,1]d on a fixed Erdős–Rényi graph with 500 nodes and mean degree 10. Each round draws a random matching of edges, evaluates the gate for every matched pair, and moves both agents of every pair that passes by μ = 0.3 toward each other, then clips to the cube. Initial opinions are independent and uniform; the graph and initial opinions come from one random stream and the matchings from another, so runs that share a seed differ only in the gate, the update step and ε. Outcomes are recorded after 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 4,000 rounds.

B. PolicySwarm as used in Section 3

120 agents on a Barabási–Albert network (m = 3) hold opinions in [−1,1]4, an ideology in [−1, 1], a fixed trust drawn from Beta(2, 2) and an influence weight proportional to their degree. Each of 60 ticks applies, in order: a policy tightening at tick 30; scheduled shocks, which move a hub group or ten random agents toward the neutral centre; a media pull scaled by ideology and trust; 150 conversation attempts under the gate, with half blocked by the deployment cap and violators' influence halved after the enforcement lag; the policy pull; and, after the lag, the pull toward the perceived policy defined in Section 3, with noise of standard deviation 0.06. The Euclidean threshold is ε = 1.0 in centred coordinates. Paired runs with and without shocks share every other random stream. The longer version lists every parameter and threshold.
